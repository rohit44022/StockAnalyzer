# Al Brooks — Trading Price Action Trends (2011) — Comprehensive Notes

## Introduction — Core Philosophy

**Zero-sum game**: Every trade has a winner and a loser. The market is controlled by institutions using algorithms. Your job is to follow what institutions are doing — never do the opposite.

**Price action is all you need**: A laptop, a 5-minute chart, and a 20-bar EMA. No indicators, no oscillators, no Fibonacci, no Elliott Wave. Indicators are derivatives of price — they add nothing that price action doesn't already show. Divergences, momentum extremes, candle patterns — all are inferior to reading bars directly.

**The fog**: Markets operate in a gray zone. Probabilities are rarely better than 60%. Concrete certainty does not exist. The best you get is 60-70% probability for brief windows during spikes.

**Institutions control everything**: ~70% of volume is algorithmic/HFT. Institutions move the market. Their footprint IS the price action. You cannot manipulate a huge market — the price action is the inescapable truth of what all participants are doing.

**Television pundits are entertainers, not traders**: Networks hire them for audience, not accuracy. 50% prediction accuracy = coin flip. Never trade based on news or pundit opinion.

**80% rule (inertia)**: ~80% of attempts to reverse a trend fail. ~80% of attempts to break out of a trading range fail. Markets resist change. This is the single most important statistical fact for trading.

## PART I: Price Action Basics (Chapters 1-12)

### Chapter 1: The Spectrum — Trends to Trading Ranges

**Everything is either trending or in a range**: At any moment, the market is either trending (one side in control) or in a trading range (equilibrium, 50-50).

**Trend definition**: A series of price changes mostly in one direction. Higher highs and higher lows = bull. Lower highs and lower lows = bear.

**Always-in direction**: At every moment, ask: "If forced to hold a position, would I be long or short?" That's the always-in direction. Trade only in that direction.

**50-50 baseline**: If you place a stop X ticks above and below the current price, both get hit ~50% of the time. Trends are brief windows where this shifts to 60-40 or better. Your job is to find those windows.

**Efficient markets**: The market quickly returns to 50-50 after any imbalance. Trends are brief — most of the time, the market is in a trading range or transitioning.

### Chapter 2: Climaxes

**What is a climax?** A move that has gone too far, too fast. It is exhaustive — the last traders on the right side have entered, and there are no more to fuel continuation.

**Buy climax**: A strong bull move (large trend bars, small tails) that exhausts buyers. Often ends with an especially large bull trend bar — the signal strong bulls and bears have been waiting for. Both sell at its close.

**Sell climax**: Mirror image for bears. Often ends with a large bear trend bar at the bottom of a protracted bear move.

**Climax bar characteristics**: Moderate-to-large size, 5-10 bars long typically, open near one extreme and close near the other, small tails.

**After a climax**: Two-sided trading begins. Dojis and overlapping bars appear. The market usually enters a trading range, not an immediate reversal.

**Consecutive climaxes**: A climax followed by another climax in the same direction. After two or three consecutive climaxes, a deeper correction is very likely (at least two legs, 10+ bars).

**Dojis signal two-sided trading**: When the market transitions from trend bars to dojis, the trend is losing momentum. Dojis are one-bar trading ranges.

### Chapter 3: Breakouts, Channels, and Trading Ranges

**Spike**: A breakout — one or more strong trend bars with little overlap, small tails. This is the always-in moment. The market is clearly one-directional.

**Channel**: After the spike, the market transitions to a channel — trending but with pullbacks and two-sided trading. The channel phase can last far longer than expected.

**Trading range**: Equilibrium. Both sides are roughly equal. 50-50 probability. Most breakout attempts fail.

**The progression**: Spike → Channel → Trading Range → Spike in opposite direction (or continuation spike).

**Flags**: Any pause or pullback in a trend is a flag. Bull flags are pullbacks in bull trends. A bull channel IS a bear flag. A bear channel IS a bull flag.

**Failed breakouts**: Most breakouts fail. This is the single most important concept for actual trading. When a breakout of a trading range fails, it sets up a trade in the opposite direction.

### Chapter 4: Follow-Through

**Follow-through is everything**: A breakout bar means nothing without follow-through on the next bar. Strong follow-through = trend is likely. Weak follow-through = the breakout may be a flag or a trap.

**Good follow-through**: Next bar continues in breakout direction, has a close near its extreme, is a trend bar.

**Bad follow-through**: Next bar is a doji, reversal bar, or goes sideways. The breakout is likely to fail or become a trading range.

**Countertrend bars in trends**: In a trend, countertrend bars look like reversal setups. They are traps. They almost always fail and become flags. 80% of reversal attempts fail.

### Chapter 5: Reversal Bars

**Bull reversal bar**: Has a close in the upper third, tail below, close above open (or small body). It signals potential reversal from a sell-off.

**Bear reversal bar**: Close in lower third, tail above, close below open. Signals potential reversal from a rally.

**Context matters more than appearance**: A reversal bar in the wrong context is meaningless. A reversal bar at a trend line after a trend line break = high probability. A reversal bar in the middle of a strong trend = almost certainly a trap.

**Best reversal bars**:
- At or near a trend line (support/resistance)
- After an overshoot of a trend channel line
- At the end of a two-legged correction
- With strong close near the extreme
- Following a prior break of the trend line

**Weak reversal bars**: Inside bars, dojis, bars overlapping prior bars heavily — these are trading range bars, not reversal bars. In strong trends, they are bull/bear flags.

**Trapped traders create the move**: When a reversal bar's signal triggers, traders on the wrong side exit at a loss. Their exit orders drive price in the direction of the reversal.

### Chapter 6: Signal Bars

**Signal bar = the bar before the entry bar**. It sets up the trade. Entry is one tick beyond the signal bar in the trade direction.

**Strong trend bars as signal bars**: A strong trend bar closing on its extreme with small tails and a close at the very top/bottom = strong signal. Especially powerful after climactic behavior.

**Weak signal bars**: Small dojis, bars in the middle of ranges, bars with large tails on both sides. They indicate uncertainty, not conviction.

**In strong trends, signal bars look BAD**: This is one of the most important lessons. Strong trends produce weak-looking buy signals that work, and strong-looking sell signals that fail. Beginners short the strong-looking sell signals and miss the trend. Experienced traders buy the weak-looking signals because they see small pullbacks in a strong trend.

### Chapter 7: Outside Bars

**Outside bar**: High above prior bar's high AND low below prior bar's low. It's a one-bar trading range.

**Trading outside bars**: The direction of the close matters most. A bull outside bar (close near top) in a bull trend = continuation. A bear outside bar in a bull trend = potential reversal, but only if context supports it.

**Inside-outside-inside (ioi)**: A common pattern. The final inside bar sets up the breakout trade.

### Chapter 8: Time Frames

**5-minute is the standard**: Brooks recommends the 5-minute chart for most trading. Smaller time frames (1-min, 3-min) have more noise and trap more traders.

**Patterns exist on all time frames**: What's a signal bar on the 5-minute is several bars on the 1-minute. What's a trend on the 5-minute might be a spike on the 15-minute.

**Don't use smaller time frames for wider stops**: A 3-minute chart may show a tighter stop, but the market doesn't respect your chart's time frame.

### Chapter 10: Second Entries

**Second entries are the highest-probability entries**: A second attempt at a move is more reliable than the first. High 2 (second attempt to go up) and Low 2 (second attempt to go down) are bread-and-butter setups.

**Why second entries work**: The first attempt often fails and traps traders. When the second attempt triggers, the trapped traders from the first attempt exit, adding fuel to the second move.

**In strong trends, even first entries work**: In a strong bull trend, a High 1 entry (first pullback) is often reliable because the trend has strong momentum.

### Chapter 11: Consecutive Trend Bars

**Multiple consecutive trend bars = strong trend**: When you see 4+ consecutive bull trend bars, the trend is very strong. Look to buy any pullback.

**Swing trading in strong trends**: When you see consecutive trend bars, swing at least part of your position. Take partial profits at 2× your risk, hold the rest with a trailing stop below the most recent swing low.

### Chapter 12: Pattern Evolution

**Patterns morph**: A pattern that fails often evolves into a larger pattern. A failed low 2 might become a wedge. A double top might become a double bottom bull flag.

**Failed patterns trap traders**: The failure of one pattern drives the market as trapped traders exit, providing fuel for the next pattern.

**Common morphing sequences**:
- Expanding triangle: 5 legs → 7 legs
- Failed final flag → breakout pullback sell setup → wedge reversal
- Double top bear flag → double bottom bull flag (especially in first hour)
- Spike and channel → trading range → double bottom bull flag

**First hour is especially fluid**: Both double tops and double bottoms commonly form, putting the market in breakout mode. Take both entries and swing part, because a big move commonly follows.

## PART II: Trend Lines and Channels (Chapters 13-17)

### Chapter 13: Trend Lines

**Bull trend line**: Below the lows. Bear trend line: above the highs.

**Most profitable trades**: Enter in the direction of the trend on pullbacks to the trend line. Continue until the trend line is broken.

**Trend line break = first sign of two-sided trading**: It does NOT mean the trend has reversed. It means control is no longer one-sided. After a break, there is usually a test of the trend extreme (higher high, double top, or lower high at a bull top).

**Each successive trend line is flatter**: As a trend matures, new trend lines have shallower slopes, indicating loss of momentum. Eventually, trend lines in the opposite direction become more important.

**Repeated tests of a trend line**: If the market repeatedly bumps against a trend line without drifting away, one of two things happens:
1. Most commonly: breaks through (reversal attempt)
2. Less commonly: accelerates away from the line (trend acceleration)

**Most breakouts of trend lines fail**: The market races toward a trend line with strong momentum. Beginners short at the trend line thinking the trend has ended. Experienced traders buy there with limit orders. The breakout below the trend line fails and the trend resumes.

**Monthly/weekly trend lines matter too**: Trend lines on all time frames can generate trades. The 1987 crash ended on a test of a monthly trend line.

### Chapter 14: Trend Channel Lines

**Trend channel line**: Opposite side from trend line, same general slope. In a bull trend: trend line below lows, trend channel line above highs.

**Overshoot and reverse**: When price overshoots the trend channel line (pokes through it) and reverses, especially on the second penetration, this is a reliable countertrend trade.

**Wedge shape = urgency**: When trend line slope > trend channel line slope, buyers are more eager (buying smaller pullbacks). This urgency creates a wedge, which often reverses.

**Slope of final flag ≈ slope of new trend**: A minor observation but interesting — the slope of the last flag before a reversal often approximates the slope of the subsequent opposite trend.

**Why reversals happen at channel line overshoots**: Strong money waits for the channel line test to exit/reverse. They sell at the close of the overshoot bar, above its high, and below the next bar's low. Weak traders see the strong bar and buy emotionally — they get trapped.

### Chapter 15: Channels

**A channel is a sloped trading range**: All channels — bull channels, bear channels, triangles, wedges — are areas contained between two lines.

**Triangles are channels**: Expanding triangle = two diverging trend channel lines. Contracting triangle = two converging trend lines. A wedge = a converging ascending or descending channel.

**Vacuum effect in channels**: As price approaches one side of the channel, traders on both sides hold back their orders (bulls wait to sell, bears wait to sell). The absence of one side creates an imbalance that sucks price quickly to the channel boundary. Then both sides act at once and price reverses back toward the other boundary.

**Bull channel = bear flag**: The market will usually break out through the bottom. Bear channel = bull flag: the market will usually break out through the top.

**Three pushes**: Most channels end after three pushes (e.g., three pushes up in a bull channel). After the third push, especially with a trend channel line overshoot and reversal bar, short aggressively.

**Tight channel = spike on higher time frame**: A very tight channel with tiny pullbacks is a strong trend. It may be a spike on a 15-minute or 60-minute chart. The broader channel that follows can reach a measured move target based on the tight channel's height.

**When to trade with trend vs. countertrend in channels**: At the beginning of a channel, trade with the trend. As it matures and develops more two-sided trading (deeper pullbacks, more overlapping bars, bear bodies in bull channel), experienced traders begin to also trade countertrend.

### Chapter 16: Micro Channels

**Micro channel**: 2-10 bars with no pullbacks. No bar trades beyond the prior bar's extreme against the trend. The strongest micro trends have consecutive trend bars without any overlap.

**Micro channel breakouts**: When a micro channel breaks, the breakout usually fails and sets up a with-trend entry. The failed breakout becomes a pullback in the larger trend.

**Trading micro channels**:
- With-trend: Enter on the failed breakout of the micro channel
- Don't short a bull micro channel until there's a clear break AND a failed attempt to resume
- In a strong bull micro channel, buy below bars on limit orders (the pullback to the prior bar's low)

**10+ bar micro channel**: When a micro channel extends beyond 10 bars, the first breakout is more likely to lead to a two-legged correction (countertrend). But even then, the correction usually leads to a resumption of the trend.

### Chapter 17: Horizontal Price Levels

**Support and resistance**: Breakouts beyond support/resistance are usually tested. If the breakout is strong, the test holds and the trend continues. If weak, the market returns into the range.

**Middle of range = magnet**: The midpoint of any trading range acts as a magnet, pulling price back. This is why most breakouts fail — the magnetic pull of the range center is too strong.

## PART III: Trends — Theory and Execution (Chapters 18-20)

### Chapter 18: How to Trade a Trend (Master Chapter)

**The single most important chapter for actual trading.**

**Stop entries (recommended for most traders)**:
1. Buy High 2 pullback to MA in bull trend
2. Sell Low 2 pullback to MA in bear trend
3. Buy wedge bull flag pullback in bull trend
4. Sell wedge bear flag pullback in bear trend
5. Buy breakout pullback after breakout from bull flag
6. Sell breakout pullback after breakout from bear flag
7. Buy High 1 in strong bull spike (NOT after buy climax)
8. Sell Low 1 in strong bear spike (NOT after sell climax)
9. In very strong bull: buy on stop above prior swing high
10. In very strong bear: sell on stop below prior swing low

**Limit entries (experienced traders only)**:
1. Buy bull spike at market, at close of every bull bar in spike, or on limit below prior bar's low
2. Sell bear spike at market, at close of every bear bar, or on limit above prior bar's high
3. Buy close of first bear bar in a bull spike
4. Sell close of first bull bar in a bear spike
5. In bull trend: buy at bull trend line or prior swing low (double bottom bull flag)
6. In bear trend: sell at bear trend line or prior swing high (double top bear flag)
7. Buy at/below weak Low 1/2 signal bar on limit in possible new bull after strong reversal
8. Short at/above weak High 1/2 on limit in possible new bear after strong reversal
9. Buy/sell on limit in quiet flag at MA
10. Buy below bull bar breaking above bull flag (anticipating breakout pullback)
11. In bull trend, buy at fixed ticks down from high (e.g., 2-4 point pullback when average range is 12)
12. Scale into trend direction as market moves against you (plan position sizes in advance)
13. In bull trend >20 bars above MA: buy at MA on limit, scale in lower
14. In strong bull: buy close of first bear bar closing below MA
15. In strong bull: a pullback is a small bear trend — buy on limit at/below a swing low in that small bear, expecting the breakout below it to fail

**The three choices**: At any moment in a trend, you can be long, short, or flat. In a bull trend, only two are acceptable: long or flat. In a bear trend: short or flat. A tiny fraction can trade countertrend profitably — assume you are not one of them.

**Why most traders lose in trends**: They enter with one plan and manage with another. Scalpers hold for swings; swing traders scalp out. Stick to your plan.

**Swing trading math**:
- Risk ~2 points (typical Emini)
- Don't take ANY profit until market moves at least 2× risk
- 60%+ probability of success in trend
- Probability × Reward must significantly exceed (1 - Probability) × Risk
- Never risk more than your reward

**Trailing stops**: After each new high in bull trend, raise stop to one tick below most recent higher low. Don't tighten to breakeven too early — trends often come back to test entry price before continuing.

**How trends end**: Selling pressure builds (larger pullbacks, more bear bars, deeper corrections). Strong bulls stop pressing above swing highs and instead take profits. Strong bears begin to initiate at new highs. The tipping point: most traders stop buying small pullbacks and start selling rallies.

**The climax at a mature trend**: After 30+ bars of trend, an unusually strong breakout bar is often exhaustive. Strong bulls and bears wait for this bar, then both sell aggressively — bulls take profits, bears initiate. Both expect at least a 10-bar, two-legged correction.

**Weak traders get trapped**: They buy the climax bar emotionally, competing against computers that don't have emotion as a variable. Their losses are inevitable.

### Chapter 19: Signs of Strength in Trends

**Bull trend signs of strength**:
1. Big bull trend bars with closes near highs, small tails
2. Consecutive trend bars
3. Two or more large trend bars with little overlap (spike)
4. Gaps (open above prior close)
5. Trend bars with close at very top = gap on lower time frame
6. No climaxes / not exhaustive
7. Countertrend moves are weak: small bars, lots of overlap, tails
8. Overshoots of minor corrections
9. Corrections are brief: 1-5 bars
10. 20+ bars above MA (20 gap bar situation)
11. Small pullbacks — Emini's average daily range is ~12 points, a pullback of only 4-5 ticks is tiny
12. With-trend bars appear slowly (grinds higher)
13. High 1 and High 2 setups work
14. Shallow two-legged corrections
15. Trend closes at extremes consistently
16. Reversal bars fail → become flags with follow-through in trend direction
17. Good-sized trend bars on 3-minute chart = with-trend inside or one-bar with-trend on 5-minute
18. As trend weakens: more two-sided trading develops

**Bear trend signs of strength**: Mirror image of all the above.

### Chapter 20: Legs and Two-Legged Moves

**Two-legged moves are the core rhythm**: Most trends have at least two legs. When a strong move occurs, expect at least a test of the extreme after a pullback — i.e., two legs minimum.

**Three-push patterns**: The most common leg structure is three pushes. The third push often overshoots and reverses. After three pushes, expect at least a two-legged correction.

**Legs within legs**: Each large leg contains smaller legs. The term "leg" applies to any smaller trend that is part of a larger trend.

**Pullback**: A temporary countertrend move. Can be as small as a single inside bar. Even an inside bar is a pullback — it represents a pause and two-sided trading on a smaller time frame.

## PART IV: Common Trend Patterns (Chapters 21-26)

### Chapter 21: Spike and Channel

**The most common and important trend pattern.**

**Structure**: Strong spike (breakout) → Channel (grinding continuation) → Trading range or reversal.

**Spike phase**:
- Always-in moment is clear
- Few bars, strong momentum, little overlap
- Both bulls and bears agree there should be little trading at these prices — market moves quickly through them
- Can be as brief as one bar

**Channel phase**:
- Two-sided trading begins
- Pullbacks appear
- With-trend entries still have edge but look weak
- Countertrend scalps possible (for experienced traders only)
- Three pushes common → third push often reverses
- Channel is a flag on higher time frame

**After channel ends**:
- Usually becomes a trading range
- 50% of the spike is the key magnet: if the market falls to the 50% level of the spike, it's a ~50-50 market (equilibrium)
- First move into the range from the channel is often followed by a second leg

**Consecutive climaxes**: A spike followed by another spike (parabolic acceleration). Usually 2-3 consecutive climaxes before exhaustion. After the final climax, expect a correction.

**Measured move targets**: Height of spike projected from end of channel, or height of channel. These targets are guidelines, not guarantees.

### Chapter 22: Trending Trading Range Days

**Structure**: Trading range forms, then breakout on one side, new trading range forms at a different level, possibly another breakout.

**Key characteristics**:
- Opening range ~50% of average daily range → breakout likely forms trending trading range
- Multiple tight trading ranges connected by breakouts
- Range acts as magnet — breakouts often pulled back into the range
- Late-day reversal is common

### Chapter 23: Trend from the Open and Small Pullback Trends (KEY CHAPTER)

**Trend from the open**: The most powerful type of trend day. The extreme (high in bull, low in bear) forms in the first few bars and the market trends all day.

**Primary characteristics**:
1. Low of bull day (or high of bear day) forms in first few bars
2. If opening range < 25% of average daily range → may have double bottom/top before trending
3. If opening range ~50% of average daily range → breakout more likely to become trending trading range day
4. May begin with strong multi-bar spike OR small opening range
5. If strong spike lasts 3+ bars from open, first pullback entry usually good for at least a scalp
6. If spike covers many points, day usually becomes spike and channel
7. Large gap → often leads to trend from open (60% in gap direction, 40% opposite for gap up)
8. 20 gap bar and MA gap bar setups come late in these trends
9. Occurs ~20% of days (~1-2 per week)

**Small pullback trend**: The STRONGEST type of trend day. Pullbacks are all < 20-30% of average daily range. Only 1-2 times per month.

**Small pullback characteristics**:
- Pullbacks are tiny (e.g., 9-12 ticks when average daily range is 12 points)
- Usually a larger pullback (~150-200% of earlier pullbacks) after 11:00 a.m. PST
- Often heralded by one or two relatively large trend bars (exhaustive climax)
- Swing setups are 70%+ probability even though they never look certain
- Many countertrend trend bars keep beginners looking for reversals
- Relatively tight channel — pullbacks hit breakeven stops, trapping traders out
- Trail stops below swing lows, NOT at breakeven

**The trap of strong trend days**: Buy setups look BAD. Sell setups look GOOD. Beginners short the sell setups and lose all day. Experienced traders see bad-looking buy signals with small pullbacks as signs of an extremely strong bull trend, and they buy relentlessly.

**Don't tighten stops to breakeven**: In a tight channel, the market will usually come back to your entry price before reaching a new extreme. Wait for a new high, THEN raise your stop to below the most recent higher low.

**7:00 a.m. PST reports**: Often create the spike that starts the trend. Computers have a huge edge on reports (process data in milliseconds). Wait 1-2 bars for always-in direction to become clear.

**First bar of the day**: Is always a signal bar for trend from the open in either direction. If it's a strong trend bar with a gap, the breakout in either direction is usually a good trade.

**If it fails**: ~40% of the time. If the market doesn't pause by bar 3-4, it may have gone too far too fast, increasing reversal odds.

### Chapter 24: Reversal Days

**Structure**: Market trends one direction, then reverses and trends the opposite direction into the close.

**Characteristics**:
- Middle of the day often has climactic behavior (strong trend bars in original direction)
- The reversal spike comes suddenly — a strong countertrend bar appears
- After the reversal spike, the old trend is often retested (lower high or double top for bull-to-bear reversal)
- Follow-through in reversal direction confirms the reversal day

**Trading reversal days**: Trade the initial trend until you see a climactic exhaustion bar. Then look for reversal signals. The first pullback after the reversal is typically a high-probability with-trend (new direction) entry.

### Chapter 25: Trending Trading Range Days (Detailed)

**Most common day type**: Trading range for hours, then a breakout, then possibly another range.

**The late-day move**: Often the breakout comes after the market has been range-bound for hours. A lazy flag in the middle of the day often leads to a breakout in the final hour or two.

**The opposite reversal**: After a trending trading range day moves one direction, the opposite extreme sometimes gets taken out over the next couple of hours. The odds are high that the reversal will at least test the midpoint of the day's range.

**Multi-day trending trading ranges**: On a daily chart, several days can form a trending trading range pattern — ranges connected by breakouts over days.

### Chapter 26: Stairs (Broad Channel Trends)

**Stairs pattern**: A channel trend with clear, measured swing points. Each leg is roughly similar in size. Two-sided trading throughout.

**Characteristics**:
- With-trend entries at swing points (buy at each higher low, sell at each lower high)
- Shrinking stairs = waning momentum → reversal likely
- Breakout of channel often leads to two-legged correction
- Momentum diminishes with each push
- Three-push patterns common → reversal after third push

**Trading stairs**:
- With-trend at swing points
- Expect test of prior swing point after each push
- If each swing is ~10-15 ticks, trail stops ~14-15 ticks from most recent swing low (just beyond the expected pullback)

## KEY RULES — Quick Reference

### The Numbers
- **80%** of reversal attempts fail
- **80%** of trading range breakouts fail
- **60%+** probability needed for a trade to be worthwhile (with equal risk/reward)
- **70%+** probability needed for scalping (risk > reward)
- **50-50** is the baseline — markets are in equilibrium most of the time
- **20-bar EMA** is the only indicator needed
- **2×** minimum reward:risk for swing trades
- **40%** of reliable patterns still fail
- **20%** of days are trend from the open days
- **1-2×/month** small pullback trend days occur

### Always-In Rules
1. At every moment, determine always-in direction
2. Trade ONLY in that direction
3. If you can't tell → market is in a trading range → use range rules (book 2)
4. Flip only after: (a) trend line break, (b) failed test of old extreme, (c) clear signal

### Entry Rules
1. **Second entries are highest probability** (H2, L2)
2. **First pullback after strong move = reliable entry**
3. **Strong trends: enter for any reason** — the trend itself is the setup
4. **Never trade countertrend** until after trend line break + failed test + signal bar
5. **Buy above bars in bull trends, sell below bars in bear trends**
6. **In strong trends, use wider stops and smaller position sizes** — adjust dollars at risk, not the trade
7. **Breakout pullbacks are reliable** — enter on the pullback after a breakout from a flag

### Management Rules
1. **Never take profit until market moves ≥2× your risk** (swing trades)
2. **Trail stops below most recent swing low** (bull) or above swing high (bear)
3. **Don't tighten to breakeven too early** — trends test entry prices before continuing
4. **After each new extreme, raise/lower stop** to below/above the most recent swing point
5. **Scale out**: Take partial at 2× risk, hold rest for trend continuation or clear reversal
6. **Exit on third consecutive climax** — expect 10-bar, two-legged correction
7. **Exit on unusually large trend bar** in mature trend (30+ bars) — it's often exhaustive

### What NOT to Do
1. **Never short in a bull trend** (or buy in a bear trend) — not until the trend has clearly reversed
2. **Never trade a reversal bar in the middle of a strong trend** — it's a flag, not a reversal
3. **Never buy/sell because "it can't go any further"** — the screen edge is an illusion
4. **Never enter on emotion** — computers don't have emotion; you lose against them every time
5. **Never use one plan for entry and a different plan for management**
6. **Never ignore the 80% rule** — most reversals fail; most breakouts fail
7. **Never short strong-looking sell signals in a bull trend** — they are bear traps
8. **Never tighten stops just because the market comes back to entry** — it will do this before continuing

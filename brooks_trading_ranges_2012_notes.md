# Al Brooks — Trading Price Action Trading Ranges (2012) — Comprehensive Notes

## PART I: BREAKOUTS — Transitioning into a New Trend

### Core Philosophy
- **Most fundamental concept**: The market is always trying to break out, then tries to make every breakout fail. Every trend bar is a breakout. Traders must constantly assess whether a breakout will succeed or fail.
- Every trend bar = breakout = spike = gap = climax (all functionally equivalent)
- Most breakouts fail. Strong resistance to change (inertia).
- A breakout is a move beyond a prior point of significance (trend line, prior high/low, prior bar high/low).

### Breakout Mechanics
- **Breakout point** → **breakout test** (pullback to that area) → **breakout gap** (space between)
- Significant breakout: large trend bar without significant tails, makes always-in position clearly long or short
- All breakouts are spikes; can be 1 or several consecutive trend bars
- **Inflection area**: setups in both directions → breakout mode → enter direction of breakout

### Three Outcomes at a New High/Low
1. **More buying** (new trend strength) — market moves far enough for scalp profit above breakout
2. **Profit taking** — market hesitates, goes sideways → bulls still bullish, buying pullbacks
3. **Strong bears dominate** — hard reversal → at least 2 legs down, ≥10 bars correction

### Breakout Entry Methods (Bull Example)
- Buy close of bar that confirms always-in long
- Buy limit at close of pause/pullback bar, tick above its low, at its low, 1-2 ticks below
- Buy limit on 1-4 tick pullback (Emini) or 5-10 cent pullback (stocks)
- Buy stop 1 tick above high of bar (or above prior swing high)
- Buy the first H1 (breakout pullback)
- On 1/2/3 minute chart: buy at/below low of prior bar, above H1/H2, limit at MA

### Context Rules
- Strong trend + breakout above prior swing high in strong trend → buy
- BUT if breakout is still below highs from left side of screen → might be trading range → only buy pullbacks
- **"Trade like a trading range until clearly in a trend"**
- A breakout in a pullback (minor trend) against the major trend → usually fails back into major trend direction

### Signs of a STRONG Bull Breakout (Ch. 2)
1. Large bull trend body, small/no tails. Larger bar = higher probability
2. Volume 10-20× average → follow-through likely, possible measured move
3. Spike goes far, lasts several bars, breaks multiple resistance levels by many ticks
4. As bar forms, price near its high, pullbacks < ¼ bar height
5. Sense of urgency — want to buy but pullback never comes
6. Next 2-3 bars also have bull bodies ≥ average size
7. Spike grows 5-10 bars without pulling back more than 1 bar
8. Move above prior swing high far enough for scalpers to profit
9. Bar low at/1 tick below prior close
10. Bar open above prior close
11. Bar close on its high or 1 tick below
12. **Micro gap**: low of bar after bull bar ≥ high of bar before bull bar → measuring gap
13. Context favors breakout (trend resumption, higher/lower low test after trend line break)
14. Several strong bull trend days recently
15. Growing buying pressure in trading range — bull bars more prominent
16. First pullback after ≥3 bars
17. First pullback lasts only 1-2 bars, not after strong reversal bar
18. First pullback doesn't reach breakout point or breakeven stop
19. Close reverses many recent closes/highs (5-20+ bars)

### Signs of a WEAK/FAILING Bull Breakout
1. Small/average body with large tail on top
2. Next bar has bear body (reversal or inside bar) closing on/near low
3. Context unfavorable (rally to range top with bear bars, overlapping bars, tails, pullbacks)
4. Market in trading range for several days
5. Next bar is strong bear reversal or bear inside bar
6. Bar after bull bar has low below high of bar before bull bar (no micro gap)
7. First pullback within 2 bars
8. Pullback extends several bars
9. Resumption stalls, forms lower high with bear signal bar
10. Breaks above resistance by only 1 tick, then reverses
11. No scalper profit possible before pullback
12. As forming, pulls back >⅔ of bar height
13. Pulls back ≥⅓ height 2+ times while forming
14. Pullback falls below breakout point, no gaps
15. Pullback falls below low of first spike bar
16. Breakeven stop hit
17. Sense of confusion

### Breakout Probability and Trader's Equation
- Strong breakout: **≥60% chance** of reaching measured move ≈ size of spike
- In a spike, the risk = distance to bottom of spike; reward = measured move up from spike
- As spike extends, earlier entries get better reward:risk
- Once strong trend underway, **every tick is a with-trend entry**
- Breakout success probability: ~60-70%. Even strongest breakouts fail ~30% of time.

### Spike and Channel Pattern
- Breakout (spike) → channel → channel usually retraced → tests back to start of channel
- Creates incipient trading range: spike top + channel bottom = range boundaries
- Channel after spike in weak trend: gap area = measuring gap = breakout gap

### Chapter 1: Trading a Breakout Example
- In strong spike: buy close of any bar (bull or bear), buy on 1-2 tick pullback
- Risk to below spike → trade small (⅓ normal size) due to large risk
- Spike of 60+ ticks with minimal pullback = strong always-in signal
- After 3rd consecutive buy climax without correction → expect ≥10-bar, 2-legged correction
- Strong bear trend bar in bull trend → buy its close (fade reversal attempt, most fail). Target: high of bar, then measured move up.

### Failed Breakouts and Breakout Pullbacks
- When breakout fails → becomes reversal setup
- When failed breakout fails → becomes breakout pullback (trend resumes)
- Compare strength of breakout vs. reversal bar: stronger wins
- If 3-bar bull spike followed by small bear bar → spike likely wins → buy pullback
- Flag breaking out in unexpected direction → measuring gap → measured move

### Late-Day Climactic Breakouts
- Final hour in strong trends: risk managers force losing traders to exit → 1-2 large climactic bars
- These plunges/surges usually don't have much follow-through (forced liquidation)
- Momentum programs amplify the move

### Chapter 3: Initial Breakout
- In strong bull spike on 5-min Emini: buy with market order, buy 2-tick pullback (visible on 1-2 min chart), or buy H1/H2
- Two-bar breakout bar patterns: bar + doji, inside bar, or trend bar + breakout reversal
- If breakout fails, creates trap → trapped traders fuel the reversal
- Institutions place enormous orders, creating imbalance → spike extends rapidly
- After spike: **60% chance** of measured move in direction of spike, if bars close strong
- Risk on initial breakout: worst case = below spike. Trade ⅓ normal size for wide stop.

### Chapter 4: Breakout Entries in Existing Strong Trends
- In existing trend, smart money doesn't wait for pullback → buys breakout above prior swing high
- Strong trends: H1 and H2 pullbacks are reliable entries; deeper pullbacks signal weakening
- Always-in position is the key: if always-in long, look to buy everything
- Trend extremes: after 3+ pushes with momentum waning, transition from always-in to two-sided begins
- In strong trend, even a bear trend bar close is a buy opportunity (trap for shorts)

### Chapter 5: Failed Breakouts, Breakout Pullbacks, and Breakout Tests
- **Failed breakout** → creates reversal. Failed reversal → creates breakout pullback (trend resumes)
- Breakout pullback = pullback that reaches area of breakout point. The gap between breakout point and pullback is the "breakout gap"
- If breakout goes 20 ticks beyond breakout point → becomes strong always-in signal
- **Breakeven stops**: In Emini, ~60% of breakouts come back to breakeven before continuing. In stocks like GOOG ($750): 10-30 cent pullback common before move continues
- Breakout of a flag: if flag breaks in unexpected direction → that flag becomes measuring gap
- After 3rd push in same direction → potential wedge → likely to fail
- **Consecutive failures**: one with-trend entry fails → creates another pullback entry. These "quiet breakout pullbacks" are reliable
- Spike and channel after breakout: channel often retraces back to spike area
- Failed breakout of tight range: usually reverses at least to other side of range
- 5% of the time: breakout fails on news spike (earnings, etc.) → immediate reversal

### Chapter 6: Gaps
- **Every trend bar = a gap** (functionally equivalent to breakout, spike, climax)
- Gap types:
  - **Breakout gap**: space between breakout point and first pullback
  - **Measuring gap**: middle of gap projects equal move beyond → measured move target
  - **Exhaustion gap**: gap late in trend (after ≥10 bars of trend) → signals potential climax reversal
- **5-10 consecutive trend bars without touching MA** → measuring gap likely between MA and price
- Middle of gap = middle of move. Project: bottom of move to middle of gap → same distance above gap = target
- **Negative gap**: when pullback goes below breakout point → difference is negative. Projections less reliable.
- **Micro measuring gap**: on 5-min chart, low of bar after trend bar ≥ high of bar before trend bar → sign of strength, measuring gap, gap test is support
- Filled gaps: if gap is filled (price returns through it), gap acted as magnet. Stronger magnetic pull as price approaches.
- Daily chart gaps: when low of today > high of yesterday (or vice versa). Middle of gap → measured move projection.
- Always-in direction confirmed by gap: gap + strong trend bar + close on extreme = strong always-in
- Exhaustion gap after ≥10 bars of trend: take profits, look for reversal, not yet an entry countertrend until prior countertrend strength shown
- Gap as sign of trend: "any trend bar is a gap" — if bar open = prior close, but close ≠ open, there's a body gap
- **Breakout gap fill rule**: if gap from breakout fills within ~20 bars, breakout has failed → potential reversal

## PART II: MAGNETS — Support and Resistance

### Core Philosophy
- Market always drawn toward magnets (support/resistance levels)
- **Inertia**: strong propensity for market to continue what it has been doing. ~40% of first attempts succeed as breakout.
- Computers drive ~70-80% of volume. All magnets have mathematical basis.
- **Vacuum effect**: when no sellers between price and magnet above, market accelerates upward (and vice versa). Vacuum = sharp countertrend move to magnet.
- Moving average is key magnet: in trend, it's support/resistance. After ≥4-5 bars away from MA → high probability of returning to it.
- Trade with trend toward magnet. Don't trade countertrend AT the magnet unless prior countertrend strength (trend line break).

### Chapter 7: Measured Moves Based on the Size of the First Leg
- **Leg 1 = Leg 2 (AB = CD)**: most common measured move. Measure first leg, project from pullback.
- In spike: **≥60% probability** of reaching measured move equal to spike height
- Spike risk calculation: risk = spike height. If spike is 4 pts, risking 4 pts to make 4+ pts with 60% probability → good trader's equation
- **Middle of spike = measuring gap midpoint**: 50-55% chance target is reached; but also incipient range boundary
- Once in trading range: probability of reaching either extreme = ~50-50
- **Spike and channel**: after spike, channel forms. Measure spike height → project from end of pullback
- Two-legged corrections: often complete a move. After 2 legs, expect protracted countertrend move that often tests back to breakout point.
- Measured moves from legs in ABC corrections: leg A low to B high, project from C low
- Projections are approximate — market often overshoots or undershoots by a few ticks

### Chapter 8: Measured Moves Based on Gaps and Trading Ranges
- Daily chart gaps (bar low > prior bar high): middle of gap → measuring gap → measured move
- Intraday: gap between breakout point and first pullback low → measuring gap
- If breakout pullback enters the gap → gap becomes smaller, but midpoint still valid for projection
- **Negative gap**: pullback goes below breakout point → negative height → less reliable projection
- Trading range height → measured move from breakout point
- **Flag projections**: after breakout from flag, project flag height from breakout point
- "Once there is a breakout flag, swing part of your with-trend trade until measured move is reached"
- **At measured move target**: begin looking for countertrend entries IF good setup + prior countertrend strength

### Chapter 9: Reversals Often End at Signal Bars from Prior Failures
- Market often reverses at prices where prior signal bars exist (areas where traders previously lost)
- If trend reverses and reaches a prior failed signal: **60% chance** of further move in reversal direction
- As market falls through range: probability drops from ~60% toward 50% (neutral) in middle of range
- Below middle: probability of further downside increases again
- Prior failed signals act as magnets and support/resistance

### Chapter 10: Other Magnets — Complete List
1. Trend lines
2. Trend channel lines
3. Leg 1 = Leg 2 measured move targets
4. Spike and channel: start of channel (tested quickly)
5. Prior day's high, low, open, close
6. Recent swing highs and lows (double bottom/top flags)
7. Breakout points
8. Gaps (all types, including MA gaps)
9. Trend extreme after each pullback type
10. Trading range extremes and midpoints
11. Final flags (breakout from flag → retrace to flag → break other side)
12. Barbwire areas
13. Entry/signal bar protective stop areas
14. Entry price (breakout test)
15. Large trend bar opposite extreme (low of huge bull bar, high of huge bear bar)
16. Common scalp targets: AAPL 50¢/$1; Emini 5-6 ticks (4-tick scalp), 3/4/10 pts (swing)
17. **Protective stop size = minimum expected move**: if stop is 12 ticks, expect at least 12 ticks in your favor
18. Daily/weekly/monthly swing H/L, bar H/L, MAs, gaps, Fibonacci, trend lines
19. Round numbers: $100, $200 in stocks; 12000, 13000 in Dow
20. Huge trend bar extremes: stops placed beyond large bars are magnets → market often runs them before reversing

## PART III: PULLBACKS — Trends Converting to Trading Ranges

### Core Philosophy
- Every pullback is a small trading range. As pullbacks deepen and grow more complex, the trend weakens.
- Pullbacks progress through a **sequence of increasing weakness**: each stage means bears are gaining more control.
- Two-legged moves are universal fractal patterns — every correction tends to be two-legged at some time frame.

### Chapter 11: First Pullback Sequence (Ordered Weakness Progression)
- In a bull trend, pullbacks grow progressively weaker in this order:
  1. **Single bar** (H1) — mildest; trend very strong
  2. **Minor trend line break** — small 2-3 bar pullback
  3. **Moving average touch** — trend still intact but slowing
  4. **Moving average gap bar** (bar entirely below MA) — first sign of real weakness
  5. **Major trend line break** — significant; trend may be ending
- Each step = bears gaining more control. Early pullbacks (H1, H2) are high-probability with-trend entries. Later pullbacks (MA gap bars, major trend line breaks) signal transition to trading range.
- **First pullback of each type is the most reliable with-trend entry**: first touch of MA after 20 gap bars, first MA gap bar, first major trend line break test.
- After the first MA gap bar: expect a test of the bar's extreme, then usually at least one more push in the trend direction.

### Chapter 12: Double Top/Bottom Bull/Bear Flags (Continuation Patterns)
- **Double top bear flag**: two pushes up in a bear trend → continuation pattern (NOT a reversal). Short below the second push.
- **Double bottom bull flag**: two pushes down in a bull trend → continuation pattern. Buy above the second push.
- These are the **most common and reliable continuation patterns** in trends.
- The second push often slightly exceeds the first (trapping countertrend traders) before resuming with-trend.
- In a bear trend: any rally that forms a double top with a prior swing high = bear flag. The failed breakout above the prior high traps bulls.
- Key rule: **in a trend, assume every reversal pattern is a continuation pattern** until proven otherwise.

### Chapter 13: Twenty Gap Bar Setups
- **20 gap bar setup**: trend so strong that market doesn't touch the MA for 20+ consecutive bars.
- The **first touch of the MA** after 20+ gap bars = very high-probability with-trend entry.
- Why it works: institutions have been waiting to add to positions at better prices. When price finally reaches MA, they buy aggressively.
- The touch often overshoots MA by 1-2 ticks, then reverses sharply in the trend direction.
- Even in the rare case where the pullback continues, traders typically get at least a scalp profit.
- After first touch: expect at least one more new extreme in the trend direction.

### Chapter 14: First Moving Average Gap Bars
- **MA gap bar**: bar with its **entire range** on the opposite side of the MA from the trend (e.g., in bull trend, a bar entirely below the MA).
- First MA gap bar = first sign the trend may be weakening into a trading range.
- **Trading rule**: after the first MA gap bar, expect a test of the bar's extreme. The extreme of the first MA gap bar often holds as support/resistance.
- If the test succeeds (holds): trend likely resumes for at least one more leg.
- If the test fails: trend is transitioning to trading range or reversing.
- Multiple consecutive MA gap bars = trend has likely ended; now in trading range.

### Chapter 15: Key Inflection Times (PST)
- **7:00 AM** — Pre-open activity; institutions position before the open
- **7:30 AM** — Market open; first 30 minutes most volatile, most reversals
- **11:00 AM** — End of morning session; trend often pauses or reverses
- **11:30 AM** — Lunch; volume drops, barbwire/tight ranges common
- **Noon** — Afternoon session begins; new trends often start
- These times are when **stop runs and traps** are most common. Institutions know where retail stops cluster and use these time windows to trigger them before the real move begins.
- Strongest trends often begin at these inflection times, especially at the open and after noon.

### Chapter 16: Counting Legs
- **Two-legged correction**: the minimum expected correction after any significant move. After two legs, look for trend resumption.
- Every move = two legs at some time frame. A "one-legged" move on 5-min is two legs on 1-min.
- **After a spike**: expect at least a two-legged correction before trend resumes.
- **After 3+ legs in same direction**: trend exhaustion likely. Expect deeper correction or reversal.
- Legs can be of different sizes — the second leg doesn't have to equal the first.
- In trading ranges: expect two-legged moves to each extreme. Buy after two legs down, sell after two legs up.

### Chapter 17: Bar Counting — H1, H2, H3, H4 / L1, L2, L3, L4
- **H1** (High 1): first bar whose high exceeds prior bar's high after a pullback in a bull trend. Only valid in **spike phase** of strong trend with no climax signs.
- **H2** (High 2): second attempt to go above prior bar's high. **Most reliable with-trend buy setup** in bull trends. Buy above H2 signal bar.
- **H3**: third attempt = wedge bull flag. Still with-trend, but weaker than H2.
- **H4**: fourth attempt. If H4 fails → trend has likely reversed. **Stop looking for with-trend entries.**
- Mirror for bear: L1, L2, L3, L4 (shorts in bear trends).
- **Context rules**:
  - H2 in bull trend = buy near top (strong) — pullback entry in trend
  - H2 in trading range = buy ONLY near bottom of range (not near top)
  - H1 reliable ONLY in spike phase of strong trend + no climax
  - Failed H2 → expect H3 (wedge) or H4; momentum of breakout determines which
  - Failed H4 → market has likely reversed
- **Signal bar quality matters**: strong trend bar in direction of trade = better. Doji or opposite-trend bar = weaker signal.
- In a strong bear trend, H1 and H2 are NOT buy setups — they are **bear traps**. Wait for trend line break before buying.

### Chapter 18: Wedge and Three-Push Pullbacks
- **Wedge pullback** (three-push pattern): three pushes against the trend → with-trend entry on first signal bar after third push.
- Wedge pullback in a trend = high-probability continuation setup. Enter on first signal.
- **Wedge reversal** (three pushes WITH the trend at its end): reversal setup, but needs **second entry** — first reversal attempt from wedge top/bottom often fails.
- Steep wedge in a tight channel: NOT a reversal signal. Tight channels continue; don't fade them.
- Three pushes can be overlapping or spread across many bars — doesn't need to look like a textbook wedge.
- Every pattern with three pushes that is sideways or sloped = functionally a triangle/wedge.
- If wedge has 3 pushes and is in a tight channel → probably continues, NOT reverses. Wait for breakout strength assessment.

### Chapter 19: Dueling Lines
- **Dueling lines**: a pullback channel line (countertrend) intersects with the larger trend's support/resistance line at the same point.
- When two lines from different contexts converge → **high-probability trade** at the intersection.
- Example: bear channel line (from pullback) meets bull trend line (from larger trend) → buy the intersection.
- The intersection creates a zone where multiple types of traders agree on value → strong support/resistance.
- One of the most reliable setups because it combines with-trend entry + countertrend exhaustion.

### Chapter 20: "Reversal" Patterns as Continuation Patterns
- **Head and shoulders in a trend = continuation pattern** (NOT a reversal). The "head" is the trend extreme, the "shoulders" form a flag.
- Treat H&S in a trend as a **wedge flag**: three pushes against the trend → with-trend entry.
- **Double tops in a bull trend** = bear flags that usually fail → continuation. Only become real tops after prior countertrend strength (trend line break).
- **Double bottoms in a bear trend** = bull flags that usually fail → continuation.
- **80% rule**: 80% of reversal attempts fail and become continuation patterns. Only 20% succeed.
- For a reversal to succeed, need: (1) prior trend line break showing countertrend strength, (2) strong reversal bar, (3) good follow-through. Without all three → probably just a flag.

## PART IV: TRADING RANGES

### Core Philosophy
- Trading ranges = uncertainty. Both bulls and bears see value at current prices.
- **80% of breakout attempts fail** in trading ranges → fade breakouts, buy low, sell high.
- Trading ranges are the hardest environment — most traders should reduce size or sit out.
- Channels are sloping trading ranges: **bull channel = bear flag**, **bear channel = bull flag**.

### Chapter 21: How to Trade a Trading Range
- **Buy near the bottom, sell near the top**. Never buy near the top or sell near the bottom.
- Use **limit orders** to enter — stop entries get you in at the worst price in a range.
- **Scalp, don't swing**: in a range, every move reverses. Take profits quickly.
- The trading range dilemma: scalping requires high probability (≥70%), but ranges rarely offer that certainty.
- **Wait for failed breakouts**: the most reliable range trade. Breakout fails → enter opposite direction.
- Second entries are more reliable: if first reversal attempt at range extreme fails, wait for second.
- When in doubt, don't trade. Wait for the range to break and a new trend to establish.
- **Final flag**: when a tight trading range forms late in a trend, it often becomes the last flag before reversal. Breakout fails → market returns through the range to the other side.

### Chapter 22: Tight Trading Ranges and Barbwire
- **Tight trading range**: bars overlap heavily, range is narrow. **Trumps everything** — no stop entries work.
- **Barbwire**: 3+ overlapping bars with at least one doji. Most dangerous pattern for stop-entry traders.
- **Rules for tight ranges/barbwire**:
  1. Do NOT use stop entries — you'll buy the high and sell the low repeatedly
  2. Wait for a breakout (large trend bar clearing the range by ≥3 ticks)
  3. Fade the first breakout bar (most breakouts from barbwire fail)
  4. Enter on the **failure of the breakout** or on the **breakout pullback**
  5. If first failure also fails → second entry in original breakout direction (very reliable)
- Consecutive reversals in barbwire → 10+ consecutive losers possible with stop entries. **Never use martingale** (doubling position size after losses).
- Tight ranges are continuation patterns ~55% of time; break in trend direction slightly more often.
- But 45-49% break opposite direction first → that failure often creates a larger trading range.
- Sloping tight trading ranges (micro channels) behave identically — still a tight range.
- **Expanding triangles** within ranges: intensely two-sided, magnetic pull draws breakouts back. Most breakouts fail.
- All tight trading ranges are magnets — breakouts get pulled back into the range.
- When tight range forms after 20+ bars of trend → often the **final flag**. Breakout fails → market returns to range price area.

### Chapter 23: Triangles
- Triangles = trading ranges with ≥3 pushes in one direction and ≥2 in the other.
- All triangles (ascending, descending, symmetrical) have the **same trading implications** — no need to distinguish.
- Break out slightly more often in direction of prior trend, but not enough to trade on that alone.
- **Every trading range with 3 pushes is functionally identical to a triangle**.
- Three-push patterns represent **stronger countertrend pressure** than 1- or 2-push pullbacks → tend to occur late in trends → often become **final flags**.
- If triangle has >5 pushes → just call it a trading range.
- **Expanding triangles**: can be reversal or continuation. First breakout often fails → forms larger expanding triangle in other direction.
- **ii pattern** = small triangle on lower time frame.
- **Wedge in a tight channel**: even with 3 pushes, if channel is tight, it continues — NOT a reversal. Wait for breakout and assess strength.
- When triangle is tall enough: trade it like a range — fade extremes, buy bottom, sell top.
- Breakout from triangle often leads to measured move ≈ height of the triangle.

## PART V: ORDERS AND TRADE MANAGEMENT

### Chapter 24: Scalping vs. Swinging
- **Scalper**: holds 1-15 minutes, exits on limit at profit target. Reward ≈ risk. Needs ≥70% win rate.
- **Swing trader**: holds through pullbacks, captures 2-4 larger swings per day. Reward ≥ 2× risk. Can profit at 40-50% win rate.
- **Key insight**: the person taking the other side of a scalp MUST be a swing trader (math requires it). If scalp has 60% success, the opposite trade has ≤40% — only profitable with larger reward.
- Scalping one-point in Emini: need >67% win rate just to break even. Most traders can never sustain this.
- **Minimum viable approach**: risk = reward (e.g., 2-point stop, 2-point target). Only take trades where you believe probability ≥60%.
- Cherry-picking the "best 3 trades": if you've waited that long for the perfect trade, you deserve more than 1 point. Go for 2-4 points.
- **Swing trading is hard because**:
  1. Setups look uncertain (40-50% probability) — traders hesitate
  2. Strong signal bars come during emotional reversals — beginners still thinking old trend
  3. Entering on pullback is scary — pullback looks like new trend against you
  4. Trends do everything to keep traders out — that's how they persist
- **OCO bracket** tip: if you can't hold through pullbacks, place profit target + stop order and walk away for an hour.
- **Position management**: exit part as scalp (1-2× risk), swing the rest with breakeven stop. Scale out at fixed intervals or at price action targets.
- Strong trends: if you believe always-in, swing at least part of your position. Don't scalp the best trends away.

### Chapter 25: Mathematics of Trading — The Trader's Equation
- **Trading is entirely about math**. All successful traders understand probabilities and the trader's equation.
- **Trader's equation**: Probability × Reward vs. (1 - Probability) × Risk. Positive = take the trade.
- **The three variables** (risk, reward, probability): when one is unusually good, the others compensate by being worse. No trade has all three perfect.
- **Edges are rarely large**: market is filled with smart people trying to take money from each other. Perfect setups disappear instantly.

#### Probability Tiers and Required Setups
- **≥70% probability** (reward ≥ ½ risk to break even):
  - Scalps in strong trends
  - But most traders cannot consistently identify 70% setups → only scalp when reward ≥ risk
- **≥60% probability** (reward ≥ risk to break even):
  - H2 pullback to MA in trend
  - L2 pullback to MA in bear trend
  - Wedge flag pullback in trend
  - Buying bottom / selling top of range (equidistant move)
  - Breakout pullback entries
- **40-50% probability** (reward must be ≥ 2× risk):
  - Reversal trades, swing trades at turning points
  - Breakout trades into/from trading ranges
  - Must take every reasonable setup — can't cherry-pick low-probability trades (math requires a basket)
- **≤40% probability**: need reward ≥ 3× risk. Only for experienced traders.

#### Directional Probability of Equidistant Move
- At any moment: **≈50% chance** market moves X ticks up before moving X ticks down (and vice versa).
- **In a trend**: probability of equidistant move in trend direction rises to **60%+**.
- **After a strong spike**: 60-70% probability of reaching a measured move equal to the spike height.
- **In a trading range**: probability of equidistant move from the middle ≈ 50%. From the edges → 60% toward the other edge.
- This is why: in trends, reward can equal risk and still profit (60%+ probability). In ranges, need to enter at extremes to get the 60% edge.

#### Practical Application
- **If risk = reward**: need ≥60% probability. This is the most common profitable setup (pullbacks in trends).
- **If reward = 2× risk**: need ≥40% probability. These are swing/reversal trades.
- **Never take a trade where probability × reward < (1 - probability) × risk**.
- 90% probability setups exist but last only an instant — the imbalance is corrected within ticks. Risk is tiny, reward is tiny.
- **One-point Emini scalps at 80% win rate**: even if achievable, slippage and commissions erode edge. Better to go for 2+ points.

### Chapter 26: Needing Two Reasons to Take a Trade
- Every trade needs at least **two independent reasons** supporting it.
- Examples: H2 at MA + wedge pullback; double bottom + trend line test; breakout pullback + measured move target area.
- One reason alone = ≈50/50. Two reasons push probability toward 60%+.
- **Avoid trades with reasons AGAINST them**: large overlapping bars at MA (tight range = no stop entries), strong counter-momentum, buying above large signal bar at bottom of range in bear trend.
- Signal bar quality: strong trend bar in trade direction > doji > opposite-direction bar.
- **When channel is tight**: wait for second signal (lower high for shorts, higher low for longs) rather than first reversal attempt.
- In strong trends: buying new lows is viable for experienced traders using limit orders + scaling in. NOT for beginners.

### Chapter 27: Entering on Stops
- **Stop entry = most reliable entry method for beginners**. Market moving in your direction as you enter.
- Entry: 1 tick beyond signal bar extreme. Stop: 1 tick beyond opposite extreme of signal bar.
- After entry bar closes: if strong trend bar in your direction → tighten stop to 1 tick beyond entry bar. If doji → keep original stop.
- **Usually takes 6 ticks beyond signal bar** to net a 4-tick scalp (Emini). Entry = 1 tick, profit = 4 ticks, limit fill = 1 tick.
- **Restriction until profitable**: beginners should use ONLY stop entries until consistently profitable.

### Chapter 28: Entering on Limit Orders
- Limit orders = countertrend to at least the short-term direction. Higher risk, requires experience.
- About **2× as many limit setups** as stop setups on any given day, but harder to execute.
- **Key limit entry situations**:
  1. Buy at/below low of prior bar in bull channel (early stages)
  2. Sell at/above high of prior bar in bear channel
  3. Buy below low 1/low 2 signal bar in bull trend (fading bear traps)
  4. Sell above high 1/high 2 signal bar in bear trend (fading bull traps)
  5. Buy/sell at close of strong trend bar in a spike (continuation)
  6. Buy/sell at breakout point on a breakout pullback test
  7. Buy at support / sell at resistance in trading ranges
  8. Buy 60-70% pullback in a trend (reward ≈ 2× risk, probability ≈ 60%)
  9. Fade strong trend bar near climax (sell climax at bottom → buy the close)
  10. Buy at MA in strong bull trend / sell at MA in strong bear trend
- **Do NOT use limit entries on strong spikes without context analysis** — a strong spike alone is not enough reason. Must assess if it's a breakout or an exhaustive climax.
- **Fading channels**: short at trend channel line, buy at trend line. Use wide enough stop for failed breakout overshoot. Scale in if goes further against.
- **When scaling into countertrend**: exit or reverse on second move against you (H2 for bears, L2 for bulls).
- Most breakout attempts above/below prior bar fail → successful limit fading tends to be slightly more reliable than stop entries, but much harder to execute.

### Chapter 29: Protective and Trailing Stops
- **Always have a stop working in the market** — mental stops get ignored when needed most.
- **Two types**: money management (fixed ticks) and price action (beyond signal/entry bar).
- **Initial stop**: 1 tick beyond signal bar. After entry bar closes with strong trend body → tighten to 1 tick beyond entry bar. If doji entry bar → keep original stop.
- **Money management stop** (Emini, 10-15 pt avg range): ~8 ticks for most trades. Adjust for large bars.
- **Trailing stop in trends**: move to below most recent swing low after market makes new swing high. Series of higher lows = bull trend intact.
- **Don't tighten too soon**: doji bars after entry are one-bar trading ranges. Market often pokes 1-2 ticks beyond them. Keep original stop until market moves several ticks in your favor.
- **Reward often equals risk**: if market forced you to risk 16 ticks (pullback depth + 1), it often rallies exactly 16 ticks in your favor. Place profit target at 1 tick less than forced risk.
- **When entering a trading range**: stop trailing stops. Instead, exit on strength near range extreme — stops below swing lows will get hit in ranges.
- **First trade of the day** often sets minimum stop size for entire session.
- **Wide stops** are valid when: scaling in on pullbacks in strong trends, fading channels, entering during large-bar climaxes. Always reduce position size to keep total risk constant.
- **If protective stop hit**: you were trapped into a bad trade. Consider reversing if context supports it (failed L2 → go long, etc.). But NOT in a tight trading range.
- Institutions are not hunting your stops — they trade against other institutions. Your stop was hit because an institution wanted to trade at that price too.

### Chapter 30: Profit Taking and Profit Targets
- All pullbacks and reversals **begin with profit taking**. As trend matures, bulls stop buying above swing highs → start taking profits there instead.
- **Scalp targets**: OCO bracket — profit target limit + protective stop. Verify position after every entry/exit.
- **Swing targets**: 2× initial risk is a good first target. Scale out: ½ at 2× risk, ¼ at 3× risk, let rest run until opposite signal.
- **Pullbacks end at confluence of support types**: MA + Fibonacci + breakout test + trend line → multiple firms buy the same area → pullback ends.
- **Should I still hold?** Imagine you're flat. Would you enter at the market with this stop? If no → exit immediately.
- **Trade management changes with every tick**: if trader's equation weakens → tighten stop or exit. If negative → exit at market immediately.
- Profit target protects you from yourself — forces you to take profits while equation is still positive. Use a working limit order, not just a mental target.
- **In trends**: exit part on strength (at/above strong trend bar closes), swing rest until opposite signal.
- **In ranges**: exit at range extreme. Don't try to swing through the opposite side.

### Chapter 31: Scaling Into and Out of Trades
- **Scaling in**: adding to position. Best when: (1) growing pullback in always-in market, (2) fading range extremes.
- **Scaling out**: exiting partial position. Most traders do this routinely — scalp part, swing the rest.
- **Key scaling-in rule**: only scale into a losing position if **big picture/always-in direction is clear** and you believe pullback is temporary.
- "Never add to a losing position" = true for countertrend trades. Institutions add to with-trend losing positions all the time (like dollar-cost averaging).
- **Scaling-in math**: if normal risk = 2 pts on 2 contracts, instead buy 1 contract with 2-pt stop, add 1 more 2 pts lower with 2-pt stop on entire position. Same total risk, better average price.
- **Exit rule when scaling in against trend**: exit or reverse on the **second move against you** (L2 for longs, H2 for shorts), especially near MA.
- **Scaling in during spikes**: in strong bear spike, short close of each bear trend bar. Stop shorting closes after first pause/doji bar. Can scale up position during spike while keeping breakeven stops on earlier entries → same total risk, much larger position.
- **Scaling in during channels**: fade swing highs/lows in channel. Exit on test of channel extreme (start of channel for countertrend, end for with-trend).
- **Never scale in against a strong trend** — if you believe market will soon be lower, it's too early to buy.
- **Entering where weak traders exit**: market often runs obvious 3-4-5 point stops to the tick, then reverses. Place limit order 1 tick inside the obvious stop level.
- **Position management when scaling**: always know your average entry price, total risk, and maximum position size BEFORE first entry. If you can't plan it → don't scale in.

### Chapter 32: Trapped Traders — Entering Where Others Are Wrong
- **Trapped in**: entry stop catches you in a bad trade (buying a bull trap, shorting a bear trap).
- **Trapped out**: protective stop kicks you out of a good trade right before it moves your way.
- **Trading the traps** (limit order setups):
  - Failed L2 in bull trend → buy at the low of the L2 signal bar
  - Failed H2 in bear trend → short at the high of the H2 signal bar
  - H1 in a non-spike bull phase → likely to fail → short at its high
  - L1 in a non-spike bear phase → likely to fail → buy at its low
  - Large bear trend bar = sell climax → buy at its close/below its low
  - Weak low 2 after consecutive sell climaxes → buy its low (expect failure)
  - H1/H2 buy in trading range (not strong spike) → short at its high
- **General principle**: when a setup looks obvious but is in the wrong context, smart money takes the opposite side. The trapped traders' forced exits fuel the move against them.
- When bear reversal bar in tight range "has nothing to reverse" → it's NOT a reversal signal. It becomes part of a two-bar reversal in the opposite direction.
- **Most traps occur at inflection times** and after patterns that "look right" but violate context rules (e.g., L2 short in a bull trend, H1 buy after weak spike).
